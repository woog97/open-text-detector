Backbone Eval Results

Updated Oct 5, 2026 · 22:09 UTC

Two rounds of training. Oct 3: six backbones compared on the full evaluation suite. Oct 4–5: an overnight Qwen3.5 LoRA sweep of about 40 runs testing learning rate, schedule, loss and training length. Start with Old vs new for the head-to-head on a common test set.

Takeaways: old vs new

Clear signals

  • The same Qwen3.5 4B got much better. All-edit recall at the held-out 1% cutoff went from 0.21 (Oct 3) to 0.78–0.79 (overnight), consistent across 5 overnight runs (3 at 20%, 2 at full length).
  • At the same full schedule, Qwen beats ModernBERT on paper_v3 and standalone rewrites (0.84–0.86 vs 0.69; 0.77 vs 0.70), with equal all-edit recall (0.78–0.82). The gaps are well beyond the seed spread seen on these metrics.
  • Cutoffs need calibration data that looks like the test text. Cutoffs from the calibration windows produced 2–23% realized FPR; cutoffs from the dev half produced 0.6–2.1%.

Likely, with some uncertainty

  • Most of the 4B jump comes from fixing the Oct 3 recipe, not from length alone. The Oct 3 run used micro-batch 1 and a loss-picked checkpoint (the earliest stage-2 epoch). Uncertainty: there is no overnight 10% run, so length (10% to 20%) and recipe changes cannot be separated.
  • The MoE is the most efficient backbone per unit of training: at 10% it matches the full-length 9B on all edits (0.80) and leads on small edits (0.35). Uncertainty: one run, one seed, trained with the older recipe.
  • The MoE retrained with the overnight recipe (20%) is the best edit detector: 0.84 all edits and 0.98 paragraph edits at 1.5% held-out FPR. Uncertainty: one seed; small edits (0.30) and standalone rewrites (0.55) are no better than the Oct 3 MoE.
  • The full-length 9B is the best on standalone rewrites (0.77) and paper_v3 (0.86). Uncertainty: one seed; its public AUROC fell to 0.93.

Inconclusive

  • Small-edit ranking between models. MoE 0.35, 9B 0.30–0.33, 4B 0.22–0.28 and ModernBERT 0.25 differ by less than the noise. The test half has about 150 small-edit sentences (48 one-sentence), so each estimate is roughly ±0.07, and across seeds the same 4B recipe varies by ±0.02–0.07.
  • Public-text comparisons. Only about 200 public human documents per half, so 1% cutoffs realize at 2.5–6% FPR and public recall is noisy.
  • Ettin is missing from these tables (incomplete saved scores).

Highest-leverage next steps (few runs left)

  1. Create sentence-scale training data: one- and two-sentence AI edits inside human paragraphs. Nothing else moved small edits, and only 2 of 24,000 training windows contain one.
  2. A full-length MoE run with the overnight recipe (about 5 hours on one H200, and it now resumes after a crash). At 20% it already leads on all edits and paragraph edits; length is what lifted paper_v3 and standalone rewrites for the Qwens.
  3. Build a calibration set from hard human text (sentences next to edits, paired originals, untouched passages from edited papers), so a deployable 1% cutoff can be fixed once and reused.
  4. If you want the length vs recipe question settled: a 10% overnight-recipe run of the 4B and 9B takes about 20 minutes each on an H200.

Takeaways: overnight sweep

Clear signals

  • A constant learning rate with no warmup is unstable at 5e-4 and above. Collapses appear on the 4B and 9B, at 20% and full length, across seeds; 1e-3 flags every human sentence.
  • With cosine decay and warmup, learning rate barely matters at 20%: 1e-4, 2e-4, 3e-4 and 5e-4 all land at 0.77–0.78 all-edit recall (seed sd about 0.01).
  • Training to full length improves paper_v3 sentences and standalone rewrites (paper_v3 0.77 to 0.84–0.89; standalone 0.51 to 0.70–0.80), on 2 seeds of the 4B and 1 of the 9B.
  • H200s train about twice as fast as A100s here (20% 4B: 39 vs 72–88 minutes).

Likely, with some uncertainty

  • Small edits are limited by training data, not the recipe. No change to learning rate, schedule, loss weight, model size or length moved small-edit recall off 0.21–0.26. Uncertainty: this is an inference; new data has not been tested yet.
  • Raising the sentence-loss weight or the head learning rate doesn't help. K (3 seeds) matched the baseline. C (head LR) was worse, but it ran on the unstable schedule, so that result is confounded.
  • 5e-4 with cosine decay is fine at 20% but worse at full length on paper_v3 (0.66) and public AUROC (0.83). Uncertainty: one full-length seed.
  • Standard AdamW does not rescue the constant schedule (G, 2 seeds). Uncertainty: it was not tested on the stable schedule.

Inconclusive

  • 9B vs 4B. Within noise at 20% (3 seeds each). At full length the 9B is better on small edits (0.26 vs 0.21–0.24) but worse on public AUROC (0.93 vs 0.97), from one 9B seed.
  • Choosing checkpoints by dev recall instead of the final epoch. Every epoch was scored but not analyzed. The learning curves look flat after about 20%, so the gain is probably small.
  • Short-span oversampling (L). It hurt paragraph edits, and small edits stayed flat. But it only reweights 1–3 sentence spans that already exist; true single-sentence spans are almost absent.
  • Standalone-rewrite recall is very noisy across seeds (sd 0.16–0.22). Don't rank arms on it.

Highest-leverage next steps (few runs left)

  1. New sentence-scale edit data is the main lever left for small edits.
  2. Spend scarce runs at full length on an H200 (about 3.5–4 hours for the 9B), with the stable recipe: 2e-4, cosine, warmup, micro-batch 8. Short runs plateau.
  3. Use dev-half cutoffs (or a hard-human calibration set) for every report, not a 0.5 cutoff or the calibration windows.

About 40 Qwen3.5 LoRA runs overnight on 4×A100 and 3×H200. Each arm changes one thing from the baseline recipe; most are 20% of the training schedule with 2–3 seeds, plus full-length learning curves. Every checkpoint is scored on a fixed 3,511-row set split into dev and test halves by paper; the table shows the test half at the final epoch. These numbers run lower than the sentence ROC section above because this set includes more human sentences right next to edits, but every run is scored on the same set.

  • Recipe changes plateau. Every stable recipe lands near 0.77 all-edit recall and 0.21–0.25 small-edit recall at 1% FPR. Learning rate (1e-4 to 5e-4), sentence-loss weight, short-span oversampling and 9B vs 4B all fall within seed noise.
  • The constant schedule, not the LoRA Without Regret learning rate, caused the collapses. At 5e-4 with cosine decay and warmup (J), results match the baseline; constant 5e-4 (B) trails or collapses, and 1e-3 flags every human sentence.
  • Training length helps paper_v3 sentences and standalone rewrites, but not small edits. Two full-length baseline seeds reach paper_v3 0.889/0.852 and standalone rewrites 0.70/0.80, against 0.77 and 0.51 at 20%; small edits stay at 0.21–0.24. Full-length 9B is best on small edits (0.26) but drops to 0.926 public AUROC. Full-length 5e-4 cosine (J) generalizes worse (paper_v3 0.66, public AUROC 0.83). Small edits look data-limited: only 2 of 24,000 training windows contain an AI span under 200 characters.
  • Speed: a 20% 4B run takes about 39 minutes on an H200 and 82 on an A100.

Calibrated operating points

Each cutoff was chosen on the separate calibration windows (195 papers, none in the evaluation set) to flag 1% of human calibration sentences, then applied unchanged to the test half. The cutoffs do not transfer. On ordinary untouched human paper passages they flag about 0.2–1.6% of sentences, close to the target. But they flag 4.5–7% of human sentences right next to AI edits, 2–5% of the matched untouched passages, and 16–29% of human paper_v3 sentences. The calibration windows contain almost none of this hard human text, so the cutoff comes out far too low. A deployable cutoff needs a calibration set that includes it.

Takeaways: Oct 3 backbone comparison

Clear signals

  • Decoders generalize better to public text than encoders: AUROC 0.90–0.92 (Qwen) vs 0.73–0.74 (ModernBERT, Ettin). Ettin and the Qwens had the same 10% budget.
  • The uncalibrated 0.5 cutoff is very conservative (0.1–0.5% human FPR), so recall at 0.5 understates what the models can do.
  • Edits are caught mainly in context: the same rewritten paragraph is caught 92% of the time next to human text, but 13% standalone (MoE).

Likely, with some uncertainty

  • The MoE was the best small-edit detector on the sentence-level ROC. Uncertainty: one seed per model and different training budgets.
  • The weak Qwen3.5 4B result was a checkpoint-selection artifact. The overnight runs confirmed it: the same backbone reaches 0.78.

Inconclusive

  • The backbone ranking beyond the MoE: single seeds, ModernBERT at full length against 10% for the rest, and loss-picked checkpoints.
  • Ettin's sentence-level results are missing because its saved scores are incomplete.
